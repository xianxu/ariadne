Letter to Alamin: NexHealth’s AI Transformation

Date: 2026-04-13 From: Xian To: Alamin Uddin, Founder & CEO

Alamin,

I want to share a proposal that I believe is existential for NexHealth. Not existential as in “nice to have for growth” — existential as in “this determines whether we’re still relevant in three years.”

The Thesis

AI has crossed a threshold. LLMs can now reliably execute multi-step cognitive tasks — not just generate text, but run loops: read context, make decisions, use tools, iterate. This isn’t the chatbot era anymore. This is a new operating system for knowledge work, and it just booted up.

Every company will be restructured around this. The question isn’t whether — it’s when, and whether we’re ahead or behind when it happens.

For NexHealth specifically: we sit at the intersection of healthcare coordination, integrations, and workflows — all domains where AI loops can replace layers of manual process. The companies that figure out how to run on AI-native workflows will operate at 3-5x the output per person and have higher quality product. The ones that bolt chatbots onto existing processes will wonder why they’re losing.

Why Piecemeal Won’t Work

The natural instinct is to sprinkle AI across the org: add a copilot here, an AI feature there, let each team experiment. This feels safe and inclusive. It will also fail.

Here’s why: AI transformation isn’t a feature. It’s a paradigm shift. It changes how work gets structured — not just how individual tasks get executed. When AI runs the process loops and humans steer, you need different:

  • Hiring criteria. You need people who can direct and evaluate AI output, not just produce output themselves. The skillset is closer to “technical editor + architect” than “individual contributor who codes fast.”
  • Performance evaluation. Output-per-person changes meaning when one person with AI leverage produces what a team of five used to. How do you evaluate someone whose primary skill is steering AI to convergence on hard problems?
  • Team structure. Small teams with AI leverage outperform large teams without it. But you can’t run a large org with one set of rules and a skunk works with another — unless you explicitly create that space.
  • Process design. Code review, sprint planning, roadmap estimation — all of these assume human-speed, human-driven execution. They become bottlenecks when the execution layer moves at AI speed.

You can’t change hiring, evaluation, team structure, and process design piecemeal across an existing org. People will resist, politics ensues — not out of malice, but because the existing system rewards existing behavior. The antibodies are real.

The Proposal: A Skunk Works

I’m proposing a small, founder-sponsored team — a skunk works — with an explicit mandate:

Prove that a 3-person AI-native team can outperform a 15-person traditional team on a real NexHealth problem.

Concretely:

  1. Pick one high-value domain. Synchronizer is a good area, particularly in the Cloud EHR where we are behind. Create a new Cloud EHR integration AI first.

  2. Staff it tiny. 2-3 people max. People who are curious, adaptable, and comfortable with ambiguity. Not the most senior — but people who can steer AI, iterate fast, while having the tool building mentality. I have Erik S. in mind for now.

  3. Give it different rules. Different hiring bar (optimize for AI-leverage aptitude, not years of experience in X framework). Different evaluation. Different process (AI-native workflows, not sprint ceremonies designed for human-speed work).

  4. Protect it. This is the critical part and the part only you can do. This team must report directly to you — not through the existing engineering org. I’ll be blunt: our current engineering leadership is not equipped to lead this. The skills required — deep intuition for AI capabilities, willingness to throw out established process, comfort with radical ambiguity. Routing this through the existing chain will produce what it’s optimized to produce: incremental improvements within the current paradigm. That’s the opposite of what we need. A skunk works dies when it gets dragged into consensus-driven decision-making with people who haven’t seen the paradigm work and don’t understand what’s possible. It needs air cover from the founder, and a direct reporting line to you, both for air cover and for your product insights.

  5. Timebox it. 90 days to show results. If a tiny team with AI leverage can demonstrably outperform on speed, quality, or both — that’s the proof point to expand. If it can’t, we’ve lost very little.

Why Only the Founder Can Do This

This isn’t something a VP or director can champion:

  • It requires changing incentive structures (hiring, perf), which is a founder-level decision.
  • It requires protecting a team from organizational and interpersonal gravity.
  • It requires making a bet on a new paradigm before the evidence is overwhelming. By the time the evidence is overwhelming, it’ll be too late.

The hardest part isn’t the technology. The model capability is here, even if the broader support harness is still maturing. The hardest part is creating the organizational space to use it properly. That’s a founder problem.

What I’m Asking

  1. Your sponsorship to stand up this skunk works.
  2. A real problem domain to prove it on — something where success is measurable and meaningful to the business.
  3. Protection from premature scaling or dilution. Let it be small, weird, and fast until it proves itself.

I’ve been building and operating with AI-native workflows intensively. The leverage is real, but it requires a fundamentally different way of working — one that the current org structure isn’t designed to support. I’d like to show you what’s possible.

Happy to walk you through a live demo of what AI-native workflows look like in practice. Seeing it is more convincing than reading about it.

— Xian
