## Thread A: The Organizational Reckoning

### Concentration of Context

The scattering problem. Today's knowledge worker has their context spread across:
- Notion for docs
- Jira for tasks
- Slack for communication
- Google Drive for files
- GitHub for code
- Email for external

Each tool has its own AI feature. None of them see the full picture. MCP bridges are the industry's answer, but they're lossy and slow — a straw connecting two swimming pools.

The super knowledge worker from post 2 concentrates context. One repo. One folder. Everything AI needs to see is *there*. That's not a preference — it's a structural advantage. The quality of AI output is directly proportional to the context it can access.

Organizations face the same choice: scatter context across 10 SaaS tools and get 10 mediocre AI features, or concentrate it and get one environment where AI actually works.

### The Management Problem

When one person operates at 10x, the surrounding org becomes the bottleneck:

- **Sprint ceremonies designed for human-speed execution become absurd.** Two-week sprints when someone can ship a feature in a day?
- **Code review as we know it breaks.** The volume of AI-assisted output overwhelms traditional review processes. New verification patterns are needed.
- **Performance evaluation loses its vocabulary.** How do you evaluate someone whose primary skill is steering AI to convergence? Our perf systems don't have words for this yet.
- **Team structure inverts.** Instead of 8-person teams with a manager coordinating, you get 2-person teams with deep AI leverage and a lightweight coordination layer.

This isn't hypothetical. I wrote about this in a letter to my CEO. The answer isn't to sprinkle AI across the existing org. It's a paradigm shift that requires a skunk works — a small team with different rules, proving the new model works before scaling it.

### Why People Will Resist

Not out of malice. People are incentivized by the current system. Managers who coordinate 15-person teams aren't excited about a world where 3 people do the same work. Senior engineers whose value is "knows the codebase" feel threatened when AI can learn the codebase in an afternoon. This is the classic innovator's dilemma, playing out inside organizations.

"That said" — the people who lean in will have outsized careers. The early adopters of this workflow become the architects of the next generation of organizations. Pretty compelling if you're the kind of person who runs toward change instead of away from it.

## Thread B: The Product Opportunity

### The Gap

Post 2's super knowledge worker is real but inaccessible. You need to be comfortable in a terminal, willing to configure your own tools, and patient enough to build up the workflow over weeks. That's a tiny market.

The opportunity: take the patterns that work (shared state, specialized harnesses, skill compounding, tweaking loops, context concentration) and make them accessible to knowledge workers who don't live in terminals.

### What the Product Looks Like

Not "AI Notion." Not another chatbot. A workspace where:

- **The folder is the workspace.** Your files, your structure, visible and ownable. Git underneath but you never type `git`.
- **AI operates surgically, not wholesale.** Highlight a paragraph, give feedback, AI adjusts. Not "generate a whole document and hope."
- **Workflows are user-authored.** "Every time I review a contract, check these 12 clauses." The system remembers. Next contract, one click.
- **Context is local and concentrated.** Everything AI needs is in the folder. Import, don't bridge.

### The Wedge

One specific workflow, fully realized, that's so obviously better people adopt the workspace to get it. Not "general-purpose AI workspace" — that pitch is too abstract.

Candidates: "The best way to write a research paper." "The best way to prepare for a deposition." "The best way to build a client deliverable." One workflow. Then they discover the compounding.

### Dogfooding

Here's the part that makes this real: we're building the company using the workflow the company sells. The pitch deck was written in it. The blog posts were written in it. The skills were built with it. If the workflow can't build its own company, it can't build anything.

This isn't a theoretical product. It's a lived practice being turned into a product. That's a pretty different starting point than "we had an idea and raised money to build it."
